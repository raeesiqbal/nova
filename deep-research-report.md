# Deep research on improving the Nova proposal UI and UX

## What the client is really asking for

The client is no longer testing whether the document *works*; he is testing whether it *looks like serious money*. The hidden brief is: make the proposal feel premium at first glance, make it feel human rather than AI-generated, and make it reassure commercial decision-makers very quickly. In other words, he wants a sales document that looks like it came from a disciplined commercial operator, not a clever automation experiment. UX research strongly supports that instinct: first impressions materially influence perceived credibility, and most readers scan for meaning rather than reading densely from top to bottom. citeturn7search0turn7search2turn7search15turn7search1

Reading the emotional subtext without pretending to diagnose him: this client sounds highly sensitive to anything that feels generic, vague, or under-finished. That makes sense for someone rebuilding trust and trying to avoid bad-fit work. So the design should signal control, seriousness, and commercial care. The proposal should feel closer to a board-facing executive memorandum than to a trade flyer: restrained, deliberate, and evidence-led. Objective, scannable language is especially important because users tend to distrust hype and “marketese,” which is exactly the tone that makes AI-written material feel cheap. citeturn7search1turn7search5turn7search2

## Design changes with the highest payoff

The highest-return change is to rebuild the first page so that it *looks expensive before it is read carefully*. Keep the dark header, but turn the opening into a composed hero section. The left side should contain the proposal title, recipient, and a short executive-summary thesis. The right side should contain the site visual the client mentioned — ideally the roof image with solar overlay — with a small caption such as “Desktop assessment, indicative only, refined after site survey.” Add a low-opacity burst watermark behind header content and a thin blue–green–gold accent rule beneath the title. That would pull the document much closer to the brand language already visible in the brochure while preserving the “management consultancy, not trade brochure” tone. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn3view0turn4view1turn4view2turn7search0turn7search2

The metric hierarchy should then stop treating all six cards as almost equal. The client explicitly wants **Year 1 Saving** to be the star, so make it a true hero card: wider, slightly taller, more breathing room, stronger number scale, and one line of business meaning beneath it. Beside it, place **Solar Payback** and **Combined Payback**. Only after those should the reader see roof area, system size, and longer-term value. That sequence answers the commercial questions in the order a prospect actually thinks: “What do I get now? When does it pay back? How big is the opportunity?” That is classic hierarchy work, and it will make the proposal feel far less AI-flat. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1turn7search1turn7search15turn7search5

The financial logic also needs one small but important clarification. The proposal currently states annual generation of 43,875 kWh, year-one savings of £6,600, and a current electricity rate of £0.24/kWh. At that tariff, 43,875 kWh implies about £10,530 of gross value, so a numerate prospect will immediately infer that only about 62.7% of generation is being monetized. That may be completely reasonable if the model assumes self-consumption limits, export treatment, losses, or non-coincident load — but the proposal should say so explicitly. Otherwise the numbers feel “calculated” but not fully explained. At the same time, the main chart compares cumulative savings only against solar cost, while the broader business story depends on understanding both solar-only and combined-investment recovery. ## Engineering rules that will keep the client happy

The finished deliverable should not be a prettier demo; it should be a prettier *system*. Convert the current populated file into the deterministic shell the client originally asked for: a top-of-file placeholder inventory comment, `{{...}}` tokens everywhere, explicit insertion comments for repeatable tables, stable IDs and class hooks, and optional `data-field` helpers only if they support the injector without complicating the markup. The goal is to improve the visible artifact without changing the automation model that the client already believes in. fileciteturn0file0

Treat print as a first-class surface, not a side effect. Use `@page` to control page size and margins, keep the strong colour blocks print-safe, and explicitly prevent awkward breaks around the hero, metrics, payback block, ROI callouts, and disclaimer. The current print CSS removes some friction, but it does not yet control page geometry tightly enough for a document whose whole job is to look reassuring when converted to PDF and emailed to prospects. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search2turn5search10turn5search6

The visible wins should be obvious: stronger first impression, clearer payback story, tighter brand alignment, less AI-sounding copy, safer legal tone, and cleaner print output — all without breaking the auto-population workflow. That framing matches the real purchase here: he is buying confidence, trust, and a document he can send proudly. citeturn7search0turn7search2turn7search1
